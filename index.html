01 — The Business Case

The gains are real, and they are measured.

This has stopped being a question of whether AI helps. Peer-reviewed research and national surveys now put numbers on it: more work resolved per hour, hours returned to every staff member every week, and revenue growth that shows up on the books. Workers using AI report saving roughly 5% of their working hours — about two hours a week, each — and the organizations capturing that aren't the ones with the biggest budgets. They are the ones that put AI inside a workflow instead of beside it.

15%more work resolved per hour by teams given AI assistance
34%gain for the newest staff — AI closes the experience gap fastest
70%of small businesses say AI increased revenue in the past year
73%say it made them more competitive against larger rivals

Sources: Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, “Generative AI at Work,” Quarterly Journal of Economics (2025); Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, The Impact of Generative AI on Work Productivity; U.S. Chamber of Commerce, Empowering Small Business; Thryv AI & Small Business Adoption Survey (2026).

Five people who each get five hours back is a sixth staff member — without a sixth salary.

02 — The Gap

Adoption is easy. Return is not.

Almost every organization has adopted AI. Most have nothing on the books to show for it. The failure is rarely the technology — it is that a tool was bought, handed out, and never wired into a real workflow with a real owner and a real measure of whether it worked.

95%of enterprise generative-AI pilots deliver no measurable return
80%of organizations report no material effect on company earnings
5%of deployments capture nearly all of the value created

Sources: MIT Project NANDA, The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business (2025); McKinsey, The State of AI.

What separates the 5% from the 95% is not the software. It is the operating system around it — workflow design, a named owner, staff training, and governance.

09 — What It Pays Back

Run the math before you spend anything.

A readiness assessment is a fixed fee in the low thousands. One additional full-time position, fully loaded, costs a multiple of that every year — and a hire adds capacity in a straight line. A workflow that removes four hours a week from five people's plates adds capacity every week it runs, does not need onboarding, and does not resign in October.

The published research is consistent about where the return actually comes from: not a smarter tool, but the same people completing more work per hour — with the largest gains going to the newest and least experienced staff. For an organization that rebuilds half its team every season, that is the finding that matters most.

So the case is not made with adjectives. It is made by baselining what a task costs you today, deploying against it, and measuring what it costs afterward. If the number doesn't move, the recommendation is to stop.

Capacity you build compounds. Capacity you hire has to be re-hired every year.

The four lines that decide it

  • Hours reclaimed × loaded hourly cost × 52 — the capacity returned to you each year
  • Work you currently turn down for lack of staff time — revenue left on the table
  • Slow replies and missed follow-ups — booked revenue that quietly leaks
  • A written policy that keeps donor and guest data out of the wrong tools — risk you stop carrying
